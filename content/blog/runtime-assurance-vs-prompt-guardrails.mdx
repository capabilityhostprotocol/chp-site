If you've shipped an AI agent, you've almost certainly added a safety system prompt, and maybe a prompt-injection classifier on the inputs. Good — those help. So the reasonable question about runtime assurance is: *does it replace those, or sit alongside them?* The honest answer: **it's a different layer, and you want both.**

## Two different questions

- **Prompt guardrails and classifiers** answer *"is this text trying to make the model misbehave?"* They operate on **words** — the prompt going in, the output coming back. They're probabilistic, they reduce noise, and they're cheap to add.
- **Runtime assurance** answers *"what is the agent actually allowed and technically able to do, and can I prove the controls held?"* It operates on **effects** — the capability calls the agent makes, where credentials, egress, and the filesystem actually get touched. It's deterministic at the boundary, and it produces evidence.

One screens the model's language. The other governs the model's reach. A capable model can be talked out of its language; it cannot be talked past a boundary that was never granted.

## Side by side

| | Prompt guardrails & classifiers | Runtime assurance (CHP Safety) |
|---|---|---|
| **Layer** | Content / intent (words) | Effect / authority (actions) |
| **Mechanism** | A prompt or a model judging text | Admission-gated capability calls + OS-level containment |
| **Failure mode** | A clever prompt talks its way through | Out-of-envelope effect is denied before it runs |
| **Determinism** | Probabilistic | Deterministic at the boundary (fails closed) |
| **Output** | A score / a block decision | A signed, offline-verifiable Safety Case |
| **Answers an auditor?** | "We had a filter" | "Here is the record the controls held — verify it yourself" |

## Why you want both

They're defense in depth, not substitutes. Screening at the input cuts the obvious attacks cheaply, so fewer reach the boundary. Runtime assurance is the backstop that holds **when screening is wrong** — because eventually it will be. The guarantee you can take to a security review doesn't come from "our classifier is good"; it comes from "the agent was not authorized or technically able to do that, and here's the proof."

Concretely, runtime assurance **governs** every action through a capability contract, **contains** material effects at the OS boundary ([OpenShell + OpenCell](/products/opencell)), **witnesses** execution independently, and **proves** it with a portable `.chpsafety` Safety Case an independent verifier re-derives from the evidence — offline, with no trust in the producer. The pattern, in full: [runtime assurance](/runtime-assurance).

## FAQ

**Do I still need prompt guardrails if I have runtime assurance?**
Yes — keep them. They cheaply cut obvious attacks at the input so fewer reach the boundary. Runtime assurance is the backstop for when they're wrong, not a replacement.

**Is runtime assurance just a better prompt-injection classifier?**
No. A classifier judges text and can be fooled by text. Runtime assurance gates and contains the *effect* an action would have, regardless of how persuasive the prompt was, and records a verifiable result.

**Does it prove the model is safe?**
No, and we won't say it does. It proves what the agent was authorized and technically able to affect, and that the declared controls held — an operational boundary plus evidence, not a claim about the model's intrinsic behavior.

**What do I actually get as the artifact?**
A portable, signed `.chpsafety` Safety Case. An independent verifier re-derives the claims from the evidence offline — you can hand it to an auditor and they check it without trusting you or calling your systems.
